    <h3>Do Household Inflation Expectations Respond to Macroeconomic Data Releases?</h3>
    <details class="abstract"><summary>Abstract</summary><p>This paper investigates how household inflation expectations respond to macroeconomic data releases. By utilizing an unexploited feature of the Michigan Survey of Consumers (MSC)—the interview dates of each respondent between June 1979 and May 2019—I identify whether respondent expectations differ within tight windows around data releases. I consider 480 monthly releases of the Employment Situation Summary (ESS), Consumer Price Index (CPI), Industrial Production (IP), and Gross Domestic Product (GDP), as well as 459 Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) meetings/calls. I find that household inflation expectations significantly respond to information about the unemployment rate and CPI inflation, suggesting salience of these variables for households relative to others considered. This includes the size and sign of the data release relative to the previous period data release. With 40 years of interviews, this paper also addresses whether the relevance of these releases differs during recessions. I find that information about the unemployment rate is particularly salient preceding, during, and following recessions. For a shorter subsample, this paper concludes with whether deviations in these releases from market expectations, as measured by the Bloomberg consensus forecasts, prompts adjustment. I find that household inflation expectations respond to deviations in IP and real GDP growth, indicating that unanticipated changes in output growth are relevant.</p></details>

    <h3>Does Consumer Sentiment Matter for Monetary Policy?</h3>
    <details class="abstract"><summary>Abstract</summary><p>When the Federal Reserve raises interest rates, does it matter how consumers feel about the economy? Using local projections instrumented with Romer–Romer monetary policy shocks and the University of Michigan's Index of Consumer Sentiment from 1978 to 2007, I find that consumer sentiment carries information about output and unemployment beyond standard economic fundamentals. A contractionary shock that raises the federal funds rate by 100 basis points at its peak lowers sentiment by roughly two index points, with the decline bottoming out after about a year and fading within two to three years. This response is largely the same across business cycles and interest rate environments, and only modestly larger when inflation is high. The reverse channel is much stronger: the mood of consumers shapes how monetary policy works. When sentiment has fallen over the past year, the same tightening depresses output and raises unemployment more quickly, with a second dip about four years later, while inflation barely responds at all. With sentiment near historic lows as policymakers fight inflation, these results suggest that rate hikes may cool the real economy faster than intended while doing little to bring prices down.</p></details>

    <h3>Household Inflation Expectations and the News</h3>
    <details class="abstract"><summary>Abstract</summary><p>This paper investigates how news coverage of specific topics shifts the distribution of household inflation expectations. This includes coverage of both traditional economic news as well as other non-traditional/fundamental topics like politics, energy, and international conflict. Since Ryngaert (2023) shows that medium-run expectations move toward perceived short-run inflation risks, the news that drives these risks matters for whether expectations remain anchored and how these perceived risks evolve over time. Exploiting a high-frequency identification strategy, I match the daily coverage of 180 Wall Street Journal topics from Bybee et al. (2021) to the interview dates of respondents in the <a href="https://www.newyorkfed.org/microeconomics/sce#/" target="_blank">FRBNY Survey of Consumer Expectations</a> between June 2013 and June 2017. Since the SCE includes density forecasts from each respondent, I measure the effect of news coverage for a specific topic on the distribution of inflation expectations by comparing individuals interviewed after relative to those interviewed right before. Since coverage begins in January 1984, I also match it to respondents in the <a href="https://www.sca.isr.umich.edu/" target="_blank">Michigan Survey of Consumers</a> using the interview dates from York (2023), extending the sample to 1984&ndash;2017 with point forecasts only. Existing studies use monthly data, a single news measure, or average expectations (Lamla and Maag, 2012; Larsen et al., 2021; Binder et al., 2025). Combining coverage across many topics with individual forecasts identifies which news moves the tails and dispersion of expectations, including when inflation itself is rarely in the news.<br>Results are forthcoming.</p></details>

    <h3>Practitioner's Guide: Interview Dates of Respondents in the Michigan Survey of Consumers</h3>
    <details class="abstract"><summary>Abstract</summary><p>The public microdata of the Michigan Survey of Consumers (MSC) record only the survey month in which each response appears, but the release files archived at the Inter-university Consortium for Political and Social Research (ICPSR) also record when each interview took place. This guide documents how to recover those interview dates from the 516 monthly ICPSR releases between January 1978 and December 2020, which come in four file formats and store the date in different variables over time, the edits needed to make the releases consistent, and how the dates are matched to the public microdata. Of the 277,642 MSC interviews from June 1979 through December 2020, 277,607 (99.99 percent) are matched to their exact interview date.</p></details>
